SpaceX's satellites are dodging crashes in space more than 1,100 times a day, fully automated, zero humans involved.

Most people assume space is so vast that satellites basically float around safely, crashes being some once-in-a-generation freak accident. That's not what's happening.

A robot system is quietly steering SpaceX's satellites out of the way of junk and other objects more than 1,100 times every single day. Over six months, that's 207,152 automated dodges. No person reviews it, no human approves anything. The system detects something getting too close, calculates the danger, and moves the satellite out of the way on its own. You never heard a word about a single one of them.

Here's the part that stops you cold: it doesn't wait for a near-miss. The threshold that triggers a dodge is a collision probability of just 3 in 10 million. That's not what most people would call a risk. SpaceX is swerving for dangers so small they'd never show up in a news alert.

The irony is right there in the numbers. SpaceX is celebrated for 207,152 automated dodges protecting its satellites from collisions. But SpaceX also launched the world's largest satellite constellation, which is most of the reason those dodges are needed. As more companies pile satellites into orbit, the daily count keeps climbing. And when this robot eventually makes the wrong call, nobody has figured out who answers for it.

Space is getting crowded fast, and the only thing standing between your internet connection and a chain-reaction crash is software making over 1,100 judgment calls a day with no human checking the work.
